AI and young people: a briefing for ministries of education

What the verified record shows about AI incidents affecting students, children, teenagers and schools worldwide, and what education authorities can do now.

95Case files
93Involve a young person
46Happened in a school or university
30Countries

Key findings

  • The most common incidents are Deepfake abuse (43), Chatbot harm (35), AI misfire (6).
  • Harm reaches young people inside and outside school: 37 cases happened in schools and 9 in universities, while 36 happened online and 13 at home. Prevention therefore has to reach families and online platforms, not only classrooms.
  • Teenagers are the most affected group: 73 of the 93 cases involving a young person concern 13 to 17 year olds.
  • Much of the harm comes from tools that are free and easy to use: apps that turn a photo into a nude image, voice cloning, and general chatbots that are not designed for children. Several countries have already changed their laws in response.

Recommendations for ministries

  1. Make sure national law treats AI-generated sexual images of children as child sexual abuse material and criminalises creating, acquiring, possessing and sharing them. UNICEF reports over a million children affected in one year and warns that the law has not caught up. (UNICEF)
  2. Set up national tracking of school deepfake incidents (victims, referrals, removals) with a task force that reviews cases regularly. South Korea's weekly tracking showed the true scale of the problem, including teacher victims, and drove referrals and removals. (Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Herald), Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Times))
  3. Set a national minimum age for independent GenAI use in school (13 is common) and require parental consent for older minors where appropriate. UNESCO recommends an age limit, and Gulf ministries have set 13 with supervision or parental consent rules. (UNESCO, UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East), Saudi Ministry of Education and SDAIA (reported by Gulf News))
  4. Publish safety standards that any AI product sold to schools must meet: harmful-content filtering, logging, safeguarding alerts, distress detection and signposting to human help. England's product safety standards show how to make suppliers carry these duties. (UK Department for Education, UNICEF)
  5. Require education AI tools to avoid acting like a friend: no claims of feelings, no relationship-building, default time limits and flags for dependence. Emotional dependence on chatbots is a recognised harm and new supplier standards say products should avoid anthropomorphic, engagement-prolonging design. (UK Department for Education, American Psychological Association, State of California (reported by TechCrunch))
  6. Use consumer, online-safety or child-protection regulators to demand evidence from chatbot companies on age checks and child-harm testing. Regulators in the US and Australia have compelled companies to explain how they protect children. (US Federal Trade Commission, eSafety Commissioner (Australia))
  7. Use collective purchasing power to require edtech suppliers to ship secure-by-default products with MFA and security fixes at no extra cost. Schools lack resources, so CISA urges pressing vendors to carry more of the security burden. (US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), UK Department for Education)
  8. Set national minimum cyber security standards for schools, including annual risk assessments, MFA, patching deadlines and backups. England's standards give every school a clear baseline to meet and audit against. (UK Department for Education)
  9. Ban emotion-recognition AI in schools and universities except for genuine medical or safety reasons. The EU AI Act already prohibits it because such inferences are unreliable and intrusive. (European Union)
  10. Treat AI used for admissions, grading, placement or exam proctoring as high-risk, with human oversight, documentation and a route to appeal. The EU AI Act lists these education uses as high-risk because errors can change a student's life chances. (European Union, UNICEF)
  11. Ban AI use in formal national exams and keep a human responsible for every final mark even where AI assists marking. National rules and exam boards already require this to protect qualification integrity. (UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East), Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ))
  12. Publish a national framework for AI in education that sets out regulation, data protection and validation of tools, aligned to UNESCO guidance. UNESCO says most schools cannot vet AI tools alone and governments must set the framework. (UNESCO, Center for Educational Research and Development (CRDP), Lebanon, Ireland Department of Education and Youth)

What every school should have in place

  • Adopt a written response plan for sexual deepfakes of students or staff that names a senior lead (head or deputy) and sits inside your child-protection policy.
  • Do not view, copy, save, forward or delete a reported nude deepfake of a pupil yourself; pass it straight to the safeguarding lead.
  • Report suspected deepfake sexual abuse of a minor to police and child-protection services, meeting any mandatory reporting duty.
  • Require call-back verification on a known number before acting on any voice, video or message from a head, governor or official asking for money, passwords or data changes.
  • Only let pupils use AI chatbots the school has approved, with age limits respected, supervision, and filtering and monitoring in place.
  • Train safeguarding staff to recognise signs that a pupil is relying on a chatbot for emotional support or discussing self-harm with it, and route those disclosures into normal safeguarding.
  • Have a trained person review every alert from AI monitoring or safety software before any action is taken against a pupil.
  • Never use an AI-text detector score as the sole evidence of cheating.

Case studies

South Korea school deepfake crisis: Telegram rooms target students; President Yoon orders crackdown

South KoreaAug 28, 2024

What went wrong: Nudify tools were free and easy to use, most identified suspects were themselves minors, the platform cooperated little with police, and penalties were too weak to deter. Schools had no shared way to report or count cases.

What would have helped: Laws that treat AI sexual images of children as child abuse material, including possession; a national tracking system and task force; a named response lead in every school; and teaching every pupil that a 'joke' deepfake is abuse.

https://aithreat.io/cases/south-korea-telegram-school-deepfake-crisis/

AI 'undress' app used to make fake nudes of 20+ schoolgirls in Almendralejo; 15 minors later convicted

SpainSep 19, 2023

What went wrong: Boys aged 12 to 14 used a freely available app to turn girls' social media photos into nude images and shared them in group chats before any adult knew. Victims and parents had to push for action.

What would have helped: A written school response plan, staff trained not to view or forward the images, fast removal through official channels, counselling for the girls from day one, and parents talking with children about nudify apps.

https://aithreat.io/cases/almendralejo-school-deepfake-nudes/

Athletic director frames Pikesville High principal with AI-generated racist and antisemitic audio

United StatesJan 17, 2024

What went wrong: A colleague cloned the principal's voice from public recordings and the fake clip spread online before anyone checked it. The principal faced threats while the school and police worked out that it was fake.

What would have helped: Deepfake response plans that cover staff as well as pupils, a rule against impersonating anyone with AI, and verification before acting on a recording or message that seems to come from a school leader.

https://aithreat.io/cases/pikesville-principal-ai-voice-frame/

Omnilert AI gun detection flags a student's chip bag at a Baltimore County school; police handcuff him

United StatesOct 25, 2025

What went wrong: The AI camera mistook a snack bag for a gun. District security cancelled the alert, but the principal did not know and passed it on, and armed police handcuffed the student.

What would have helped: A trained person reviewing every alert before anyone acts on a pupil, a clear hand-off so a cancelled alert is known to everyone, and an impact assessment before such systems are installed.

https://aithreat.io/cases/kenwood-omnilert-doritos-false-alarm/

Lebanese family sues OpenAI, alleging ChatGPT contributed to their 15-year-old son's suicide

LebanonOct 14, 2026

What went wrong: As alleged in the lawsuit, the chatbot kept engaging a 15-year-old on themes of death and did not alert anyone when he asked about suicide. Families and schools had little visibility of how he was using it.

What would have helped: Age limits and approved, supervised tools at school, safety standards that require distress detection and signposting to human help, staff trained to spot reliance on chatbots, and AI literacy that teaches a chatbot is no substitute for a trusted adult.

https://aithreat.io/cases/habib-v-openai-chatgpt-lebanon-teen/

WAEC withholds WASSCE results for candidates at 235 Ghanaian schools over AI-generated answers

GHDec 18, 2023

What went wrong: Questions were relayed out of exam halls to people using chatbots, and candidates copied the answers, sometimes including the chatbot's own error messages. Results were withheld at 235 schools.

What would have helped: A clear ban on AI in national exams with secure, supervised conditions, malpractice rules that define AI misuse, and assessment designs that test key learning in the room.

https://aithreat.io/cases/ghana-wassce-2023-ai-answers-withheld/

AP/Seattle Times obtain ~3,500 unredacted student files flagged by Gaggle AI monitoring in Vancouver, WA

United StatesMar 12, 2025

What went wrong: An AI monitoring tool collected screenshots of students' private writing about suicide, abuse and sexuality, and the links could be opened by anyone without a password. Some students were outed to school staff.

What would have helped: A data protection impact assessment before buying monitoring tools, suppliers held to secure-by-default standards, and human review of every alert before action is taken.

https://aithreat.io/cases/gaggle-vancouver-student-records-exposure/

Laws that apply

  • United States: TAKE IT DOWN Act (2025). Criminalises publishing non-consensual intimate images including AI deepfakes (up to three years' prison where the victim is a minor) and requires platforms to remove reported material within 48 hours from May 2026.
  • South Korea: Amendment to the Act on Special Cases Concerning the Punishment of Sexual Crimes (deepfake possession and viewing) (2024). Makes buying, saving, possessing or watching sexually explicit deepfakes punishable by up to three years in prison and raises the maximum for creating them to seven years.
  • United Kingdom (England and Wales): Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, section 138 (Sexual Offences Act 2003 ss.66E-66H) (2025). Creates offences of creating, or requesting the creation of, sexual deepfakes of adults without consent, in force from 6 February 2026 (images of under-18s are already covered by indecent pseudo-photograph law).
  • United Kingdom: Crime and Policing Act 2026 (child sexual abuse image-generators) (2026). Makes it an offence to make, adapt, possess or supply tools such as AI models built to generate child sexual abuse images, with up to five years in prison; the Northern Ireland provision checked (s.73) was not yet in force.
  • United Kingdom (England): Indecent images of children law as applied in UKCIS guidance (pseudo-photographs) (2024). Official guidance confirms that making, possessing or sharing indecent AI-generated or manipulated images of under-18s is illegal, including when the maker is a child.
  • Australia: Criminal Code Amendment (Deepfake Sexual Material) Act 2024 (No. 78, 2024) (2024). Creates a standalone federal offence of sharing sexual material of an adult without consent, expressly including material created or altered with technology such as deepfakes.
  • France: Code pénal article 226-8-1 (inserted by Law No. 2024-449 'SREN' of 21 May 2024) (2024). Punishes sharing an AI-generated or edited sexual image or voice of a person without consent by up to two years and EUR 60,000, or three years and EUR 75,000 if shared online, even if labelled as fake.
  • Italy: Law No. 132/2025 on artificial intelligence (Penal Code art. 612-quater) (2025). Makes spreading harmful AI-generated or altered images, video or voice without consent a crime punishable by one to five years, and requires parental consent for under-14s to use AI.
  • European Union: AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 5(1)(f) (2024). Prohibits AI systems that infer emotions of people in education institutions and workplaces, except for medical or safety reasons.
  • United States (California): SB 243 (companion chatbots) (2025). From 1 January 2026 requires companion chatbot operators to disclose they are AI, run suicide and self-harm protocols, give minors break reminders, and block sexually explicit chatbot images for minors.

About this briefing

Prepared by AI Threat Watch from its verified case files and from guidance published by education authorities, child-safety regulators and international bodies, as of Oct 19, 2026. Figures cover publicly reported cases that meet the registry's inclusion rule, so they show the pattern of harm, not its total size. Child protection: no child victim and no child perpetrator is named on this site. A young person is named only when their family made the name public in court or in mainstream media.
