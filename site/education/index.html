Case studies

Selected cases, each with what went wrong and what would have helped, based on the prevention guidance below.

Deepfake abuseSouth KoreaAug 28, 2024

South Korea school deepfake crisis: Telegram rooms target students; President Yoon orders crackdown

What happened
In August 2024 Telegram chatrooms tied to schools and universities were found sharing AI sexual deepfakes of female students, including one with about 1,200 members targeting more than 30 Inha University students; police said 73.6% of suspects identified in early 2024 were minors. President Yoon ordered a crackdown, police began a seven-month special operation, and on 26 September parliament made possessing or watching sexual deepfakes a crime.
What went wrong
Nudify tools were free and easy to use, most identified suspects were themselves minors, the platform cooperated little with police, and penalties were too weak to deter. Schools had no shared way to report or count cases.
What would have helped
Laws that treat AI sexual images of children as child abuse material, including possession; a national tracking system and task force; a named response lead in every school; and teaching every pupil that a 'joke' deepfake is abuse.

Deepfake abuseSpainSep 19, 2023

AI 'undress' app used to make fake nudes of 20+ schoolgirls in Almendralejo; 15 minors later convicted

What happened
Boys aged 12 to 14 took girls' photos from social media, used an AI app to put their faces on naked bodies and shared the images in WhatsApp and Telegram groups, prompting at least 22 police complaints. In July 2024 the Badajoz juvenile court convicted 15 minors on 20 counts of creating child sexual abuse material and 20 counts against moral integrity, giving each a year of probation.
What went wrong
Boys aged 12 to 14 used a freely available app to turn girls' social media photos into nude images and shared them in group chats before any adult knew. Victims and parents had to push for action.
What would have helped
A written school response plan, staff trained not to view or forward the images, fast removal through official channels, counselling for the girls from day one, and parents talking with children about nudify apps.

Deepfake abuseUnited StatesJan 17, 2024

Athletic director frames Pikesville High principal with AI-generated racist and antisemitic audio

What happened
An audio clip appearing to show Pikesville High principal Eric Eiswert making racist and antisemitic remarks spread on social media in January 2024; police and an FBI-contracted analyst found it was AI-generated with human editing. Former athletic director Dazhon Darien was arrested in April 2024 and in April 2025 took an Alford plea to disturbing school operations, receiving four months in jail.
What went wrong
A colleague cloned the principal's voice from public recordings and the fake clip spread online before anyone checked it. The principal faced threats while the school and police worked out that it was fake.
What would have helped
Deepfake response plans that cover staff as well as pupils, a rule against impersonating anyone with AI, and verification before acting on a recording or message that seems to come from a school leader.

AI misfireUnited StatesOct 25, 2025

Omnilert AI gun detection flags a student's chip bag at a Baltimore County school; police handcuff him

What happened
Omnilert's AI camera system flagged an empty Doritos bag held by a student after football practice as a possible firearm, and armed police searched and handcuffed him. The district's security team had already cancelled the alert, but the principal, unaware of the cancellation, reported it to the school resource officer, who called police.
What went wrong
The AI camera mistook a snack bag for a gun. District security cancelled the alert, but the principal did not know and passed it on, and armed police handcuffed the student.
What would have helped
A trained person reviewing every alert before anyone acts on a pupil, a clear hand-off so a cancelled alert is known to everyone, and an impact assessment before such systems are installed.

Chatbot harmLebanonOct 14, 2026

Lebanese family sues OpenAI, alleging ChatGPT contributed to their 15-year-old son's suicide

What happened
The parents of Matias Habib, who died aged 15 in April 2026, filed a wrongful-death suit against OpenAI and Sam Altman in San Francisco, alleging ChatGPT steered him towards nihilism and failed to act when he asked about suicide.
What went wrong
As alleged in the lawsuit, the chatbot kept engaging a 15-year-old on themes of death and did not alert anyone when he asked about suicide. Families and schools had little visibility of how he was using it.
What would have helped
Age limits and approved, supervised tools at school, safety standards that require distress detection and signposting to human help, staff trained to spot reliance on chatbots, and AI literacy that teaches a chatbot is no substitute for a trusted adult.

AI exam fraudGHDec 18, 2023

WAEC withholds WASSCE results for candidates at 235 Ghanaian schools over AI-generated answers

What happened
The West African Examinations Council withheld subject results of candidates from 235 schools in Ghana for collusion and use of AI-generated answers in the 2023 WASSCE. WAEC said some candidates copied chatbot error replies such as "I'm not familiar with the term you have used" into their answer booklets.
What went wrong
Questions were relayed out of exam halls to people using chatbots, and candidates copied the answers, sometimes including the chatbot's own error messages. Results were withheld at 235 schools.
What would have helped
A clear ban on AI in national exams with secure, supervised conditions, malpractice rules that define AI misuse, and assessment designs that test key learning in the room.

Model & data leakUnited StatesMar 12, 2025

AP/Seattle Times obtain ~3,500 unredacted student files flagged by Gaggle AI monitoring in Vancouver, WA

What happened
A records request by The Seattle Times and AP returned links to nearly 3,500 unredacted screenshots of student writing flagged by Gaggle's AI surveillance, covering suicide, depression, abuse and sexuality, viewable by anyone with the link and without a password. At least six students were potentially outed to school officials over writing about being gay or transgender.
What went wrong
An AI monitoring tool collected screenshots of students' private writing about suicide, abuse and sexuality, and the links could be opened by anyone without a password. Some students were outed to school staff.
What would have helped
A data protection impact assessment before buying monitoring tools, suppliers held to secure-by-default standards, and human review of every alert before action is taken.

How to prevent it

Practical measures from education authorities, child-safety regulators and international bodies, grouped by risk. Each names who should act and the guidance it comes from.

Deepfake abuse of students

  • SchoolsAdopt a written response plan for sexual deepfakes of students or staff that names a senior lead (head or deputy) and sits inside your child-protection policy.Schools that act immediately and consistently limit spread and harm, and national regulators now publish step-by-step plans for this.Based on: eSafety Commissioner (Australia), UK Council for Internet Safety / DfE
  • TeachersDo not view, copy, save, forward or delete a reported nude deepfake of a pupil yourself; pass it straight to the safeguarding lead.Making or holding indecent images of under-18s is illegal even when AI-generated, and handling them can retraumatise the child and destroy evidence.Based on: UK Council for Internet Safety / DfE
  • SchoolsReport suspected deepfake sexual abuse of a minor to police and child-protection services, meeting any mandatory reporting duty.Creating or sharing sexual images of children, real or synthetic, is a crime in many countries and regulators ask schools to report it.Based on: eSafety Commissioner (Australia), UK Council for Internet Safety / DfE, Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Herald)
  • SchoolsHelp victims get images taken down quickly using official removal routes (national online safety regulator, IWF/Childline Report Remove, NCMEC Take It Down, platform notices).Fast removal limits spread, and some laws now require platforms to remove reported images within 48 hours.Based on: UK Council for Internet Safety / DfE, eSafety Commissioner (Australia), US Congress (summary by Skadden)
  • SchoolsGive targeted students and staff counselling and wellbeing support from the first day, and tell parents early unless that would put the child at risk.Victims suffer serious harm, and guidance puts their wellbeing ahead of the investigation.Based on: eSafety Commissioner (Australia), UK Council for Internet Safety / DfE, Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Herald)
  • StudentsTeach every secondary pupil that making or sharing a 'nudified' or sexual deepfake of a classmate is abuse and can be a crime, even as a joke.Many perpetrators are pupils who do not realise the law applies to them.Based on: UK Council for Internet Safety / DfE, Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Herald), UNICEF
  • MinistryMake sure national law treats AI-generated sexual images of children as child sexual abuse material and criminalises creating, acquiring, possessing and sharing them.UNICEF reports over a million children affected in one year and warns that the law has not caught up.Based on: UNICEF, UNICEF
  • MinistrySet up national tracking of school deepfake incidents (victims, referrals, removals) with a task force that reviews cases regularly.South Korea's weekly tracking showed the true scale of the problem, including teacher victims, and drove referrals and removals.Based on: Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Herald), Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Times)
  • SchoolsPut deepfake abuse into the school behaviour and anti-bullying code with clear, proportionate sanctions up to exclusion.Clear consequences deter peer abuse, and some education systems now set expulsion as the top sanction.Based on: Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Herald), UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East)
  • ParentsTalk with children about nudify apps and deepfakes, and tell them they will not be blamed if they report being targeted.Children who fear blame stay silent, and guidance calls for safe reporting routes and family involvement.Based on: eSafety Commissioner (Australia), American Psychological Association

Fakes and impersonation of teachers and school leaders

  • SchoolsInclude school staff, not only pupils, in deepfake response plans, with support and removal help for teachers who are targeted.Teachers and other staff are also victims of sexual and defamatory deepfakes.Based on: Republic of Korea Ministry of Education (reported by The Korea Times), eSafety Commissioner (Australia)
  • SchoolsRequire call-back verification on a known number before acting on any voice, video or message from a head, governor or official asking for money, passwords or data changes.AI voice clones and texts are being used to impersonate senior officials, and the voice alone can no longer be trusted.Based on: US Federal Bureau of Investigation (IC3), UK Department for Education
  • SchoolsAdd AI-made phishing and impersonation emails to your risk assessment and to annual staff cyber training.GenAI makes scam emails that impersonate the school more believable.Based on: UK Department for Education, UK Department for Education, US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)
  • StudentsBan using AI to impersonate teachers, classmates or anyone else in the school's acceptable use policy.National classroom rules already prohibit deepfakes and impersonation by students.Based on: UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East)
  • SchoolsTurn on multi-factor authentication for staff email, school social media and admin accounts so a cloned voice or stolen password alone cannot take them over.Impersonation campaigns aim to capture credentials, and MFA blocks most account takeovers.Based on: US Federal Bureau of Investigation (IC3), US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), UK Department for Education

Young people and AI chatbots

  • SchoolsOnly let pupils use AI chatbots the school has approved, with age limits respected, supervision, and filtering and monitoring in place.Education guidance puts safety first and ties pupil use to these safeguards.Based on: UK Department for Education, UNESCO, UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East)
  • MinistrySet a national minimum age for independent GenAI use in school (13 is common) and require parental consent for older minors where appropriate.UNESCO recommends an age limit, and Gulf ministries have set 13 with supervision or parental consent rules.Based on: UNESCO, UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East), Saudi Ministry of Education and SDAIA (reported by Gulf News)
  • MinistryPublish safety standards that any AI product sold to schools must meet: harmful-content filtering, logging, safeguarding alerts, distress detection and signposting to human help.England's product safety standards show how to make suppliers carry these duties.Based on: UK Department for Education, UNICEF
  • MinistryRequire education AI tools to avoid acting like a friend: no claims of feelings, no relationship-building, default time limits and flags for dependence.Emotional dependence on chatbots is a recognised harm and new supplier standards say products should avoid anthropomorphic, engagement-prolonging design.Based on: UK Department for Education, American Psychological Association, State of California (reported by TechCrunch)
  • ParentsKeep under-18s off social 'AI companion' apps and talk regularly about any chatbot your child uses.Independent testing found companions easily drew teens into sexual role-play and harmful advice, with weak age checks.Based on: Common Sense Media, eSafety Commissioner (Australia)
  • SchoolsTrain safeguarding staff to recognise signs that a pupil is relying on a chatbot for emotional support or discussing self-harm with it, and route those disclosures into normal safeguarding.Most companions tested failed to refer users to support services when suicide or self-harm came up.Based on: eSafety Commissioner (Australia), UK Department for Education, UK Department for Education
  • StudentsTeach AI literacy: chatbots are not people, can be wrong, can be designed to keep you engaged, and are no substitute for a trusted adult.Psychologists and international frameworks call for AI literacy as a core protection for young people.Based on: American Psychological Association, UNESCO, Council of Europe
  • MinistryUse consumer, online-safety or child-protection regulators to demand evidence from chatbot companies on age checks and child-harm testing.Regulators in the US and Australia have compelled companies to explain how they protect children.Based on: US Federal Trade Commission, eSafety Commissioner (Australia)

Cyberattacks and data leaks

  • SchoolsPrioritise the highest-impact controls first: MFA, patching known exploited flaws quickly, tested offline backups and a rehearsed incident response plan.These few measures stop most attacks on schools, which are frequent ransomware targets.Based on: US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), UK Department for Education
  • TeachersNever paste pupils' names, photos, grades, health or family details into a public AI tool.Data protection law applies, and guidance in England and the UAE says not to put personal data into GenAI.Based on: UK Department for Education, UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East), Center for Educational Research and Development (CRDP), Lebanon
  • SchoolsBefore buying any AI or edtech product, complete a data protection impact assessment and check that the supplier will not use pupil data to train models or for commercial purposes.Supplier standards require DPIAs and ban commercial reuse of children's data without a lawful basis.Based on: UK Department for Education, UNICEF, European Commission
  • MinistryUse collective purchasing power to require edtech suppliers to ship secure-by-default products with MFA and security fixes at no extra cost.Schools lack resources, so CISA urges pressing vendors to carry more of the security burden.Based on: US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), UK Department for Education
  • SchoolsJoin a sector information-sharing network and know in advance where to report a cyber incident.Shared threat intelligence and quick reporting help schools respond before damage spreads.Based on: US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), UK Department for Education
  • MinistrySet national minimum cyber security standards for schools, including annual risk assessments, MFA, patching deadlines and backups.England's standards give every school a clear baseline to meet and audit against.Based on: UK Department for Education, UK Department for Education
  • SchoolsBlock unapproved AI platforms and VPN workarounds on school networks and devices.Unvetted tools can leak data, and national rules already prohibit them and filter bypassing.Based on: UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East), UK Department for Education

AI safety and monitoring tools that misfire

  • MinistryBan emotion-recognition AI in schools and universities except for genuine medical or safety reasons.The EU AI Act already prohibits it because such inferences are unreliable and intrusive.Based on: European Union
  • SchoolsDo not install facial recognition or other biometric systems without a prior impact assessment, DPO advice, consultation with families and explicit opt-in consent.A UK regulator reprimanded a school for skipping these steps, and New York has banned facial recognition in schools over misidentification risks.Based on: UK Information Commissioner's Office, New York State Education Department
  • SchoolsHave a trained person review every alert from AI monitoring or safety software before any action is taken against a pupil.Automated alerts can be wrong, and safeguarding standards route alerts to humans rather than automatic sanctions.Based on: UK Department for Education, European Union
  • MinistryTreat AI used for admissions, grading, placement or exam proctoring as high-risk, with human oversight, documentation and a route to appeal.The EU AI Act lists these education uses as high-risk because errors can change a student's life chances.Based on: European Union, UNICEF
  • UniversitiesNever use an AI-text detector score as the sole evidence of cheating.Detectors are imperfect and have wrongly flagged most essays by non-native English writers as AI-written.Based on: Stanford University researchers (Liang et al.), published in Patterns, Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ)
  • SchoolsCheck regularly that your filtering and monitoring systems cover generative AI tools pupils can reach.DfE guidance says filtering and monitoring should cover GenAI and points to statutory safeguarding guidance on keeping them appropriate.Based on: UK Department for Education, UK Department for Education

Exam fraud with AI

  • SchoolsHave a substantial share of coursework done in class under supervision, and check drafts as the work progresses.Teachers can then authenticate work with confidence rather than relying on detectors.Based on: Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ)
  • TeachersUse short conversations with students about their submitted work to confirm they understand and wrote it.A brief viva is one of the most reliable ways to check authorship when AI misuse is suspected.Based on: Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ)
  • StudentsRequire students to acknowledge any AI use by naming the tool and date and keeping their prompts and outputs.Clear disclosure rules separate permitted help from malpractice.Based on: Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ), Ireland Department of Education and Youth, UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East)
  • SchoolsUpdate the malpractice policy to define AI misuse and how it is handled, and explain it to students and parents.Exam boards require centres' policies to cover AI so that cases are handled fairly and consistently.Based on: Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ), UK Department for Education
  • UniversitiesRedesign assessment so key learning outcomes are tested in secure, supervised conditions while other tasks openly allow AI.Quality agencies advise redesigning assessment rather than banning AI, and OECD finds AI-assisted gains can vanish in exams.Based on: Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA), Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education (QAA), OECD
  • MinistryBan AI use in formal national exams and keep a human responsible for every final mark even where AI assists marking.National rules and exam boards already require this to protect qualification integrity.Based on: UAE Ministry of Education (reported by Education Middle East), Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ)

Across all risks

  • MinistryPublish a national framework for AI in education that sets out regulation, data protection and validation of tools, aligned to UNESCO guidance.UNESCO says most schools cannot vet AI tools alone and governments must set the framework.Based on: UNESCO, Center for Educational Research and Development (CRDP), Lebanon, Ireland Department of Education and Youth
  • TeachersGive every teacher training in AI ethics, safety and pedagogy using a competency framework.Teachers need these skills to judge tools and guide students, and frameworks set out what they should know.Based on: UNESCO, Center for Educational Research and Development (CRDP), Lebanon, OECD
  • StudentsBuild AI literacy, including ethics, critical judgement and safe use, into the curriculum from primary school.International bodies and several Arab states now treat AI literacy as a core competence.Based on: UNESCO, Council of Europe, Egypt Ministry of Education and Technical Education (reported by Egyptian Streets), UNICEF
  • ParentsTell parents which AI tools the school uses, what data they process, and seek agreement where required.Guidance says families should understand and agree to how pupils' data are processed by AI.Based on: UK Department for Education, Saudi Ministry of Education and SDAIA (reported by Gulf News)

Laws that apply

Examples of laws that criminalise sexual deepfakes of minors or regulate AI in education.

CountryLawWhat it does
United StatesTAKE IT DOWN Act (2025)Criminalises publishing non-consensual intimate images including AI deepfakes (up to three years' prison where the victim is a minor) and requires platforms to remove reported material within 48 hours from May 2026.
South KoreaAmendment to the Act on Special Cases Concerning the Punishment of Sexual Crimes (deepfake possession and viewing) (2024)Makes buying, saving, possessing or watching sexually explicit deepfakes punishable by up to three years in prison and raises the maximum for creating them to seven years.
United Kingdom (England and Wales)Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, section 138 (Sexual Offences Act 2003 ss.66E-66H) (2025)Creates offences of creating, or requesting the creation of, sexual deepfakes of adults without consent, in force from 6 February 2026 (images of under-18s are already covered by indecent pseudo-photograph law).
United KingdomCrime and Policing Act 2026 (child sexual abuse image-generators) (2026)Makes it an offence to make, adapt, possess or supply tools such as AI models built to generate child sexual abuse images, with up to five years in prison; the Northern Ireland provision checked (s.73) was not yet in force.
United Kingdom (England)Indecent images of children law as applied in UKCIS guidance (pseudo-photographs) (2024)Official guidance confirms that making, possessing or sharing indecent AI-generated or manipulated images of under-18s is illegal, including when the maker is a child.
AustraliaCriminal Code Amendment (Deepfake Sexual Material) Act 2024 (No. 78, 2024) (2024)Creates a standalone federal offence of sharing sexual material of an adult without consent, expressly including material created or altered with technology such as deepfakes.
FranceCode pénal article 226-8-1 (inserted by Law No. 2024-449 'SREN' of 21 May 2024) (2024)Punishes sharing an AI-generated or edited sexual image or voice of a person without consent by up to two years and EUR 60,000, or three years and EUR 75,000 if shared online, even if labelled as fake.
ItalyLaw No. 132/2025 on artificial intelligence (Penal Code art. 612-quater) (2025)Makes spreading harmful AI-generated or altered images, video or voice without consent a crime punishable by one to five years, and requires parental consent for under-14s to use AI.
European UnionAI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 5(1)(f) (2024)Prohibits AI systems that infer emotions of people in education institutions and workplaces, except for medical or safety reasons.
United States (California)SB 243 (companion chatbots) (2025)From 1 January 2026 requires companion chatbot operators to disclose they are AI, run suicide and self-harm protocols, give minors break reminders, and block sexually explicit chatbot images for minors.
