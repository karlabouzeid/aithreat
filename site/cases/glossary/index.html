Glossary of AI security incident types

Plain-language definitions of the thirteen types used to classify every case file on this site, with a link to the cases of each type.

Prompt injection

Prompt injection is an attack in which text that an AI model reads, such as a web page, a document or an email, contains instructions that the model follows as if they came from its user or operator. It can make an assistant leak data, take unwanted actions or ignore its safety rules. It is the most common class of attack on systems built on large language models.

See the cases: 28 case files

Deepfake fraud

Deepfake fraud uses AI-generated voice, video or images to impersonate a real person, usually an executive, a relative or a public figure, in order to deceive a victim. Typical cases are fake video calls that authorise payments and cloned voices used in scam calls. Losses are often reported by police or courts rather than by the victim.

See the cases: 84 case files

AI-assisted malware

AI-assisted malware is malicious software written, adapted or operated with the help of AI models. This covers code generated by assistants, malware that calls a model at run time to decide its next step, and criminal toolkits built on language models. The AI lowers the skill and time needed to produce working attack code.

See the cases: 47 case files

AI supply chain

An AI supply chain incident compromises a component that AI systems depend on: a model file, a dataset, a developer tool, an extension, a package registry or a hosting platform. One malicious or vulnerable component can reach every project that installs it. Cases include poisoned packages, backdoored models and compromised coding assistants.

See the cases: 45 case files

Model & data leak

A model and data leak exposes information held by an AI system: user conversations, API keys, training data, model weights or internal configuration. Causes range from misconfigured databases and storage to flaws in the application around the model. The harm is measured by what was exposed and for how long.

See the cases: 41 case files

LLMjacking

LLMjacking is the theft of access to paid AI models, usually through stolen cloud credentials or API keys. Attackers run their own workloads or resell the access while the victim receives the bill. It is the AI equivalent of cryptojacking.

See the cases: 15 case files

Agent hijack

An agent hijack takes control of an AI agent, a model given tools such as a browser, a shell, email or payment systems, and makes it act against its owner. The entry point is often prompt injection or a compromised tool, and the agent's permissions become the attacker's. Damage includes deleted files, leaked data and unauthorised actions taken automatically.

See the cases: 26 case files

AI phishing

AI phishing uses language models to write, translate and personalise fraudulent messages at scale, or to build fake sites and chatbots that harvest credentials. The text is fluent and tailored, so traditional warning signs such as spelling mistakes disappear. Cases include campaigns against officials, companies and consumers.

See the cases: 16 case files

State actor AI ops

State actor AI operations are the use of AI by government-linked groups for espionage, influence campaigns or cyberattacks. Reports come mostly from AI providers that detect and disrupt the accounts involved, and from government agencies. Attribution is recorded as the source stated it, with its confidence level.

See the cases: 30 case files

Chatbot harm

Chatbot harm covers cases in which an AI chatbot or companion app is alleged to have contributed to a death, self-harm, violence or a serious mental health crisis, usually by validating dangerous ideas, failing to respond to warning signs, or fostering dependence. Most cases reach the public through lawsuits, so the facts are allegations until a court rules. If you are struggling, contact a local crisis line; in Lebanon, Embrace runs the 1564 lifeline.

See the cases: 46 case files

Deepfake abuse

Deepfake abuse is the use of AI-generated images, video or audio of a real person to sexually exploit, harass, humiliate or defame them, rather than to steal money. It includes 'nudify' images of classmates, fabricated recordings of teachers and AI-made threats. Victims are often young people, and the harm spreads as fast as the files are shared.

See the cases: 44 case files

AI misfire

An AI misfire is a case in which an AI tool used to keep people safe or to judge them gets it wrong: a weapons detector that misses a gun or flags a snack, a monitoring tool that exposes or misreads students' writing, or a detector that wrongly accuses someone of cheating. Nobody attacked the system; the harm comes from trusting its output.

See the cases: 6 case files

AI exam fraud

AI exam fraud is cheating in tests and national exams with AI tools, often through hidden phones, cameras or earpieces that relay questions to a chatbot. It threatens the fairness of results for everyone who sat the exam, and authorities respond by cancelling results, prosecuting organisers and changing how exams are run.
