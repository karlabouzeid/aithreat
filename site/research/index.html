Research vs reality: AI risk warnings checked against real incidents

AI safety researchers have published detailed warnings about how AI could cause harm. This page takes each warning and checks it against the verified case files on this site: has it happened, are there early signs, or is there nothing on record yet? It compares six institutes, listed alphabetically and given equal space: Apollo Research, the Center for AI Safety, the International AI Safety Report, METR, the MIT AI Risk Repository and the UK AI Security Institute.

What the research did not foresee

22% of verified case files (95 of 433) match none of the warnings from the research sources above. The MIT taxonomy is left out of this count because it is built to cover every kind of AI risk. Much of the research deals with catastrophic or future risk, so everyday harm such as fraud is often outside its scope by design. But that is where most documented damage happens today.

About the sources

Each warning on this page paraphrases one risk from a source and cites the section, and the page where the source has pages. The MIT AI Risk Repository is a map of every kind of AI risk rather than a set of warnings, so each of its seven domains is one entry, used to check which parts of the AI risk landscape the registry covers.

How we match: a case file is linked to a warning when what happened is a real-world instance of the risk the source describes, judged by the editors from the facts in the case file. A match means the case fits the warning, not that it proves the source's wider argument. Allegations in lawsuits are labelled as allegations. Statuses are editorial and reviewed as new cases arrive. The matching is recomputed on every update. Data: Systematic coverage in the registry starts in 2025, so a first-case date shows when the registry first recorded the risk, not when it first happened. research.json (CC BY 4.0)
